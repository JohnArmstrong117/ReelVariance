How much do viewers disagree?

Controversy Score measures how spread out viewers’ ratings are. When most viewers give a movie similar ratings, its Controversy Score is low. When ratings range from “hated it” to “loved it,” the Controversy Score can be much higher.

How it works. We use a statistic called variance to measure how far ratings tend to fall from a movie’s average rating. To score a genre, we average the Controversy Scores of the movies in that genre.

Chart methods

Budget and revenue come from TMDB, and a film is plotted only when both are greater than zero. Profit is revenue minus budget. Controversy Score is the sample variance of MovieLens ratings (stars²). IMDb review scores are divided by 2 so every rating on the site uses the same 0.5–5 star scale. The profit line chart averages films inside fixed controversy bins with equal film weight. The budget scatterplot plots every financed film; while All is on, each dot takes the color of its film’s first-listed genre; selecting additional genres highlights matching films (a multi-genre film takes the color of its first selected genre) and fades the rest rather than filtering them out. Dashed trend lines are ordinary least-squares fits: one for all financed films, plus one per selected genre (needs at least two films at different budgets); the button under the scatterplot toggles them. The rating bars group films by rounded average MovieLens star rating. The radar shows mean profit and mean budget per genre on one shared dollar scale, with tick labels on one spoke. Multi-genre films contribute to every selected genre series. Missing TMDB money is left out rather than treated as zero.

A LITTLE CONTEXT

What does disagreement look like?

01

Average = overall opinion

The mean adds all star ratings and divides by the number of ratings. It hides how those ratings are distributed.

02

Variance = spread of opinion

We use sample variance: sum of squared distances from the mean, divided by n − 1. Higher values indicate a wider spread, not necessarily two opposing camps. Units are stars squared.

03

Distribution = the fuller picture

Check the histogram. A single cluster and two opposing clusters can tell very different stories. Sample size matters; a minimum-count filter is a convenience, not a confidence guarantee.

Data and methods

Films in this catalog have at least 1,000 MovieLens ratings. Rating submissions run through the end of the ml-32m dataset. Profit charts include a film only when TMDB lists both a budget and a revenue. Review words cover the IMDb reviews that link to those films.

Ratings are MovieLens half-star scores from 0.5 to 5.0. Controversy Score is the sample variance of those ratings. Genre bars average film-level means and variances equally. Violin plots pool rating counts. The time chart uses the year each rating was submitted, and each film’s yearly bins sum to its lifetime histogram. Multi-genre films belong to every listed genre.

Budget and revenue come from TMDB. Profit is revenue minus budget. Films missing either number stay in the rating views and drop out of the profit charts. Review words come from the IMDb Spoiler Dataset, joined with IMDb ids rather than titles. The language slider uses IMDb’s 1–10 review scores, which are a different scale from MovieLens stars. Counts are precomputed after common English and film words are removed. Spoiler reviews are included as word counts only; the review text is not shown.

MovieLens user ids are anonymous. Ratings can show disagreement, but not why people disagree. F. Maxwell Harper and Joseph A. Konstan (2015), The MovieLens Datasets: History and Context.
